**A year of free buses: Kellingford's busier buses and unchanged roads**

Twelve months ago, Kellingford became the largest town in the region to scrap bus fares altogether. Since then every journey on its 22 routes has been free, for residents and visitors alike, at a cost to the council of £4.1 million a year, paid for by moving money from parking revenue and a small rise in council tax. The first full year of figures, published last week, shows a scheme that has succeeded spectacularly at one of its goals and barely moved the needle on another.

The success is easy to see at any stop in the town centre. Bus journeys rose from 9.2 million in the year before the change to 12.3 million in its first year, an increase of 34 per cent. "We said people would use the buses if we took away the cost, and they have," said Councillor Aisha Bennett, the council's cabinet member for transport. "Buses that used to run half empty at eleven in the morning are now full."

The harder question is who those new passengers are. A survey of 2,400 passengers carried out for the council by researchers at Northgate University found that 41 per cent of the extra journeys were made by people who would previously have walked or cycled. Only 19 per cent were made by people who would otherwise have driven. The remaining 40 per cent were trips that would not have happened at all: older residents going into town more often, teenagers travelling to activities after school, and people visiting friends across town.

"Free fares are very good at filling buses and much less good at emptying roads," said Dr Martin Coles, the transport economist who led the survey. "If your goal is to get people out of cars, a fare cut on its own is an expensive way to do it."

The traffic figures bear him out. Car traffic on the town's five main corridors fell by 3 per cent over the year, a change Dr Coles describes as "within the range we see from one year to the next for reasons that have nothing to do with buses". Nitrogen dioxide readings at the town-centre monitoring station fell by 6 per cent, but the report cautions that the operator replaced a third of its fleet with electric buses during the same period, and that the new buses probably account for much of the improvement.

Supporters of the scheme argue that judging it on traffic alone misses the point. The survey found that households with an income below £20,000 a year made 52 per cent more bus trips than before, the largest increase of any income group. For Sheila Mott, a 71-year-old retired school cook who lives on the edge of the town, the change has been simple. "I go into town twice a week now instead of twice a month," she said. "I used to count the coins before I decided whether I could go."

Local businesses have also noticed. A survey by the Kellingford Chamber of Commerce found that footfall in the town centre was up 8 per cent on the previous year, and two thirds of the shop owners who responded said free buses had helped. The chamber's chair, Priya Desai, is careful about the numbers. "Footfall has gone up and the buses are part of that," she said. "I wouldn't claim they are the whole story. We also had a good Christmas."

Not every town-centre business is convinced. The owners of the two private car parks near the market square say their income fell by around a tenth over the year, and taxi firms report fewer short daytime fares. Neither group has called for the scheme to end, but both have asked the council to publish more detail on where the extra bus journeys begin and end, so that the effect on different parts of the town can be judged.

The scheme has had growing pains. Without cash fares to collect, buses spend about 20 per cent less time at each stop, which has helped keep timetables steady even as passenger numbers rose. But route 7, which links the hospital, the further-education college and the main housing estates, has struggled with crowding at peak times, and passengers have reported being left at stops because buses were full. The operator has added 11 buses to the network, and the council's contract payments have risen as a result. Drivers' representatives say there have been fewer arguments about fares, but more about crowding.

The scheme's sharpest critic on the council is Graham Pryce, who leads the opposition group. "We are spending four million pounds a year so that people who used to walk can ride," he said. "Nobody begrudges a pensioner a free trip into town, but we already had a concessionary pass for that. A targeted scheme for people on low incomes would cost a fraction of this." Mr Pryce also points to Dr Coles's own estimate that each car journey taken off the road has cost the council about £7, a figure he calls "extraordinary value for nobody".

Dr Coles himself resists being drawn into the politics. "The number is a fair one if you think the scheme is about traffic," he said. "If you think it's about access, about who gets to take part in the life of the town, then you need a different measure, and on that measure the scheme looks much better." He adds that a single year is a short time to judge. Travel habits change slowly, and people who start using the bus now may give up a second car in two or three years' time, which a one-year survey cannot capture.

There is also the question of what would replace free fares if they ended. Councillor Bennett points out that reintroducing fares would bring back cash handling and slower boarding, and that the scheme's administrative savings, about £600,000 a year in ticketing costs, would be lost. Mr Pryce counters that those savings are small next to the scheme's total cost and could be kept with contactless-only fares.

The council will vote in March on whether to extend the scheme for a further three years. Officers have set out three options: keep all fares free; keep fares free outside the morning and evening peaks only, which they estimate would save about £1.3 million a year; or return to paid fares with a new low-income pass. Councillor Bennett says she favours the first option but is "open to a conversation about the peaks", particularly if the money saved could pay for more buses on route 7.

Whatever the council decides, the Kellingford trial has given other towns something they have rarely had: a year of real figures rather than projections. Its message is not a simple one. Free buses filled up quickly, gave people on low incomes and older residents more freedom to travel, and seem to have helped the town centre. They did not, at least in their first year, take many cars off the road. For towns considering the same move, the lesson may be to decide first which of those things they are paying for.
