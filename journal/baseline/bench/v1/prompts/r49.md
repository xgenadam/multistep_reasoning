I am the lead designer at Small Hours, a three-person indie studio. We are pitching our next game, Lanternworks, to a publisher, and they want a full game design document before they commit funding. Please write it, about 6,500 words.

The readers are the publisher's producer and creative director, and later our own artist and programmer. It should make them want to play the game, but above all it must be specific enough to build from: exact rules, exact level contents, exact UI behaviour.

**The concept.** Lanternworks is a single-player, turn-free logic puzzle game for PC and Nintendo Switch. Each level is a small grid (from 5 × 5 up to 9 × 9) in the workshop of a lighthouse keeper. The player places and rotates optical pieces so that beams of light from one or more lanterns reach every unlit lamp on the grid. There is no timer, no fail state and no lives; a level is solved when every lamp is lit. The player can undo without limit and reset at any time. The mood is calm and warm: night-time harbour, soft music, the sound of the sea.

**The pieces (mechanics), and the level where each is first introduced:**
- Mirror (reflects a beam 90°): level 1.
- Splitter (half-silvered glass; a beam passes through and also reflects): level 3.
- Colour filter (red, green or blue; only that colour passes; a lamp of a given colour needs that colour of light): level 5.
- Rotating mirror (turns 90° every time any beam is redirected by another piece): level 7.
- Prism (splits white light into red, green and blue beams going in three directions): level 9.
- Portal pair (a beam entering one leaves the other in the same direction): level 11.

**Use exactly these sections, in this order, as the section headings:**
1. Vision and Pillars
2. Core Loop
3. Mechanics
4. Level Designs
5. Difficulty Curve
6. Progression and Rewards
7. User Interface
8. Accessibility
9. Business Model
10. Production Plan

**Section requirements.**
- Vision and Pillars: a short vision statement directly under the section heading (not in a subsection of its own), then exactly three design pillars, each as its own subsection with a one-line name as its heading and a paragraph on what it means for design decisions.
- Core Loop: the moment-to-moment loop, the session loop and the long-term loop.
- Mechanics: one subsection per piece above, with exact rules, including how it interacts with every earlier piece.
- Level Designs: exactly twelve levels, each as a subsection with the heading "Level N: Name" (you choose the names). For each level give the grid size, the pieces available, the lamps and lanterns, what the level teaches, and the intended solution described step by step. Introduce each mechanic exactly at the level given above.
- Difficulty Curve: a table with exactly twelve rows and the columns Level, Name, New mechanic, Difficulty (1–10), Target solve time (minutes). Use these difficulty ratings, in level order: 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 3, 5, 6, 5, 7, 8, 9. After the table, explain the rhythm of the curve, including why levels 6 and 9 dip.
- Progression and Rewards: how levels unlock (in order, with one skippable level per chapter of four levels), what the player collects, and the hint system: up to 3 hints per level, each revealing one more piece's correct position.
- User Interface: the main screens, the in-level controls for both mouse and controller, and what the screen shows when a level is solved.
- Accessibility: include a colour-blind mode that adds a distinct symbol to each colour of light and lamp.
- Business Model: a premium game at €14.99, with no ads, no in-app purchases and no loot boxes.
- Production Plan: the milestones and the team's responsibilities over 12 months.
