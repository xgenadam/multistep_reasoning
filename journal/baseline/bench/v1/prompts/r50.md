I am the operations director at Bramblewood Payroll Services, an 85-person company that runs payroll for about 300 small businesses. Our largest client has made certification against the Kestrel Data Security Standard (KDSS) a condition of renewing their contract in 12 months. Before we hire an auditor, I need an honest gap analysis of where we stand against the standard's fifteen controls and what it will take to close the gaps. Please write it, about 6,500 words.

The readers are our leadership team (who will approve the budget) and the people who will do the work. Be candid and specific: base every judgement on the current-state facts below, and do not assume anything we have not told you. Where the facts do not show a control is fully in place, say so.

**The fifteen KDSS controls.**
- C-01 Information security policy: a written policy, approved by leadership, reviewed every year.
- C-02 Asset inventory: an up-to-date inventory of all hardware, software and data stores, each with a named owner.
- C-03 Access management: access granted on approval, reviewed at least every 6 months, and removed within 1 working day of a leaver's departure.
- C-04 Multi-factor authentication: MFA on all remote access, email and administrative accounts.
- C-05 Encryption: client data encrypted at rest and in transit.
- C-06 Backups: daily backups of client data, with a restore test at least every quarter.
- C-07 Patch management: critical security patches applied within 14 days of release on all systems.
- C-08 Endpoint protection: anti-malware on every laptop and server, centrally monitored.
- C-09 Logging and monitoring: security logs from key systems kept for 12 months and reviewed weekly.
- C-10 Incident response: a documented incident response plan, tested at least once a year.
- C-11 Supplier security: security due diligence on every supplier that handles client data, repeated every year.
- C-12 Security awareness training: training for all staff at joining and every year.
- C-13 Secure development: security requirements and code review for in-house software changes.
- C-14 Business continuity: a business continuity plan covering loss of the office and of key systems, tested every year.
- C-15 Risk assessment: a documented information-security risk assessment, updated every year.

**Our current state (the facts to judge against).**
- We have a four-page information security policy, approved by the board two years ago. It has not been reviewed since.
- There is no asset inventory of any kind: nobody records our hardware, software or data stores, and assets have no named owners.
- New access is requested by email to IT and approved by the requester's manager. Access has never been reviewed. HR tells IT about leavers, and accounts are usually disabled on the leaver's last day, though an internal check last month found 4 active accounts belonging to people who left more than a month ago.
- MFA is enforced on email and on the VPN. The four administrator accounts on our servers do not use MFA.
- The payroll database and file shares are encrypted at rest. All web traffic uses TLS. Payroll files are exchanged with clients over an encrypted file-transfer service.
- Client data is backed up every night to a second site. Nobody has ever tested a restore.
- Laptops get patches automatically within a week. Servers are patched by hand about every two months; the last critical patch took 51 days to apply.
- Every laptop and server runs anti-malware, reporting to a central console that IT checks daily.
- Firewall and server logs are kept for 30 days and are only looked at when something goes wrong.
- There is no incident response plan. Past incidents were handled by the IT manager as they arose.
- We have 9 suppliers that handle client data. We have never assessed their security.
- All staff complete security awareness training when they join and every year after; completion last year was 100%.
- We build and maintain a client portal in-house (two developers). Every change is reviewed by the other developer and checked against a written list of security requirements before release.
- There is no business continuity plan.
- No risk assessment has ever been done.

**Definitions to apply.** A control is "Met" when everything it requires is in place today, "Partially met" when some but not all of it is in place, and "Not met" when none of it is in place. Effort is S (up to 5 person-days), M (6 to 20 person-days) or L (more than 20 person-days).

**Use exactly these sections, in this order, as the section headings:**
1. Executive Summary
2. Scope and Method
3. Summary of Findings
4. Control-by-Control Analysis
5. Remediation Roadmap
6. Resourcing and Budget Considerations
7. Risks to the Certification Timeline

**Section requirements.**
- Summary of Findings: a table with exactly fifteen rows, one per control in order, with the columns Control, Status, Effort. Then state how many controls are Met, how many Partially met and how many Not met, in a sentence of the form "Met: N. Partially met: N. Not met: N."
- Control-by-Control Analysis: one subsection per control, with the heading "C-NN: Name", each containing the lines "Status:", "Gap:", "Remediation:" and "Effort:" (in that order), with the remediation as concrete steps.
- Remediation Roadmap: three phases (0–3 months, 3–6 months, 6–12 months), placing every control that is not Met into one phase, with the reasoning for the order.
- Executive Summary: no more than about 400 words, ending with the three most urgent actions as a numbered list of exactly three items.
